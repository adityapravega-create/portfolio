WHY AERODYNAMICS

From an inverted wing to the whole car

I want to bridge the gap between CAD and the actual car and extract every ounce of performance, because every curve has the power to dictate performance.

I grew up in a family of engineers. My father is a mechanical engineer, and my first toys were the things he taught me with: LEGO, mechanics sets and model planes. In the garage I watched him take engines apart and explain every subsystem, and together we built RC cars and aircraft. In sixth grade I wrote on my report card, “I will be an engineer.” Then I found Formula 1. The idea that an inverted wing could glue a car to the ground felt like magic, and it still does. Aerodynamics became the place where science meets artistry.

At VIT Vellore I went through Pravega Racing’s interviews and tests to join the aerodynamics department of one of India’s first Formula Student combustion teams. As Aerodynamics Engineer on PRV 23, I designed the aero package (front wing, rear wing, cooling ducts and bodywork) from concept CAD through CFD to carbon parts. The first lesson was to start from the car, not the part: lap simulation tells you what aero is worth, the engine sets the drag budget, and the balance decides where the downforce has to go.

The second lesson came away from the screen. I set up simulations to look like the track, with rotating wheels, a moving ground and pitch, roll and yaw runs, and built aeromaps with Vehicle Performance so the car would run where the aero stays predictable. I modelled flaps the way they would actually be made, designed the moulds, laid up carbon by hand through the night and went out testing after dark while the campus slept. When my coast-down test put the car’s drag within a few percent of the CFD, I knew what I enjoy most: closing the gap between the model and the car.

As CFD Head on PRV 25, I led the package for a lower-powered engine: a new rear-wing slat working with the biplane, whiskers to keep the tyre wake off the rear wing, flaps on the sides of the nose that turn front-wing flow into upwash for the rear wing, ram-air sidepods for cooling and an underfloor–diffuser concept. An endplate gurney taught me to judge parts together: it added wing drag but cut the front tyre’s drag by as much. I automated our repeat CFD runs, wrote procedures so results did not depend on who ran them, and taught newer members; after handing over, I mentored the next intake through Formula Bharat 2025, where the team won Engineering Design and Cost.

Static events mattered to me as much as the car. I wrote and defended our design reports in front of the judges at Formula Student Germany and FS East, and in 2023, at our first international competition in four years, I led the cost report team without senior guidance.

At Imperial College London I completed the MSc in Advanced Aeronautical Engineering. My thesis with ESDU took me down to the equations: I extended a Python Euler solver to rotating flow as an independent check on Fluent, and traced a mismatch with theory to a normalisation slip and a dropped term. As Simulations Engineer for Imperial Formula Racing, I built the IFR-27 front and rear wing assemblies in CATIA V5, inside the regulation boxes and around the chassis pickups. Placements at Adani Defence and Aerospace, where I contributed to a loitering munition’s wing and control surfaces, and Tata Motors widened the picture.

That is why this role appeals to me. A Junior Aerodynamics Design Engineer works where I have been working: between the aerodynamicist’s idea and the part that is built and run, where a surface has to be right in CFD, possible to make and inside the rules. Active aerodynamics in the 2026 regulations makes that link tighter still, and the gap between simulation and reality (crosswind, track temperature, wear) is the one I most want to keep closing. I want to learn how McLaren turns concepts into surfaces for the wind tunnel and the track, and to bring the habits Formula Student gave me: think about the whole car, design for manufacture, and check everything against the real thing.

Ayrton Senna said, “If you no longer go for a gap that exists, you are no longer a racing driver.” For an aerodynamicist, the gap is the one between the model and the car, and I intend to keep going for it.
